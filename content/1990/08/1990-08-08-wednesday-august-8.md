This was our last full day at the coast. We were up just after 7, pushed in the pull-out, packed, and were at the Apple Peddler restaurant by 8. We headed north with only one planned stop, Seaside, before ending up in Astoria—134 miles—by 4, to be sure we get our rooms. It’s tough. There’s far more to see and do than we have time for. Devil’s Punch Bowl, Cape Foulweather, Haystack Rock, Cape Meares. We pass through tiny settlements like Oceanside, Cannon Beach and Tillamook, with interesting shops lining the highway. The road winds, speeds are often reduced to 30 mph. Jersey cow country. Valleys, lush slopes, green meadows, grey-shingle farm houses and barns. All the milk flows to Tilamook. We stopped there at 11, halfway to Astoria, for ice-cream. The place had a lot of sitting capacity, but far more were waiting for a table to become free. Even the parking lot was crowded. People standing with their cones, people in lines for deli-food, for take-out ice-cream, people taking self-guided tours of the cheese factory past empty tubs, sealed vats, a vacuum packaging machine. Too many people, too suddenly, after days of the openness, adventure, and ocean. We regrouped at the lobby. Mama thought she would like lunch here. But having just had ice-cream, no-one was hungry. We put the meal off until Seaside. We’d be there in an hour. We passed through Bayside where the expansive Nehalem Bay meets the Nehalem River. Fishing boats sitting everywhere in active pursuit of a catch. The highway from this point on was iffy, impatient drivers passing on sharp turns and narrow bridges, cyclists crowding the narrow shoulders. We were in Seaside by 1, found a road to the city centre, parked a block from the Promenade, watched a bi-bicycle with the two riders sitting side by side, a bike seating four, a bike-buggy with a steering wheel, a bicycle with two seats projecting over a centre bar, which much have required special skills. We walked along the Promenade. Two miles of condos and historic homes fronting an expansive beach where post-hole equipment was busy preparing for volleyball posts, five in a row, at least a dozen rows, for an upcoming tournament. The breeze was fresh, the sun clouded over, yet people were on the beach in bathing suits and light shirts. Papa insisted on pictures being taken at the Turnaround. We visited a central mall, a mix of restaurants, kiosks, shopping stalls, arcades, where I spent my birthday money on a wind breaker. Peter saw it first, insisted on me buying it, and was quite pleased with himself when I left the store with it in the bag. We ate at a restaurant nearby. Erik found a toy-gadget store with toy kits, wind-up toys, yo-yos, water pistols. He played with everything, asked for everything he played with, and resisted when the time came to go. We waited for the others to join us, returned along the Promenade to the van, drove up and down a few streets. Lots of homes dating to the founding of the resort.

The drive to Astoria was short. Matthew read comics, showing snippets to Cheryl’s mom while her father slept. Erik slept. Peter stretched out on the middle seat. We crossed the Youngs Bay bridge onto the narrow peninsula between the Youngs and Columbia Rivers, into Astoria. We left the road at the end of the bridge and turned into the lot of the Bayfront Hotel, backing onto Pier 3 with piles of lumber waiting to be shipped. After checking in we drove into town. We parked and split up. Peter went with Papa, ‘Because’, explained Peter, ‘Papa and I like to look at the same things.’ The rest of us walked to the harbour front, followed the riverwalk past a couple of tug boats to look and a Marina Supply store to the Maritime Museum where the Columbia, a retired coastguard vessel, is moored. It is closed to sightseers. We paid quarters into binoculars to look ocean freighters anchored in the river. We spotted a lighthouse way up the hill behind us. We agreed to meet at the van by 6. We had twenty minutes. We climbed three steep blocks past historic homes, a couple of churches and lodges, a daycare, opera house, and were at the van before Peter and Papa. Erik and I went into Chris’ Bookstore. It had nearly everything. Erik wanted nearly all of that. We didn’t stay long. We returned to the van, drove to the Astoria Column at the highest point of land. This took us past some beautiful homes. The boys and I climbed the 168-step spiralling stairway—Peter counted, Erik more than kept pace—to an observation deck with a 360° view over hundreds of miles of ocean, river, mountain and forest. We saw Cheryl far below. We waved. She waved back. Peter photographed the bridge. By the time we were at bottom my thighs were aching. We drove back to the harbour and found the Dutch Cup restaurant. We weren’t hungry but ordered anyway: sole, stacked sole, chicken strips, hamburgers, salad plate, oysters, clam chowder and fresh-ground coffee. All this while watching boats on the massive Columbia river. We stayed for two hours. Our last supper, so to speak, for this was the end of Papa’s treat.  From now on we’re on our own.

We returned to the hotel. The laundry room was closing at 9. Cheryl and Mama wanted to wash a couple of loads. The boys were in bed by 8:45. They didn’t go to sleep, making sudden wakeful movements, sounds, giggling, sitting up, in spite of desperate warnings from the parents.
