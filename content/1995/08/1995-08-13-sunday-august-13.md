Another summer is nearly over.

We spent the first part on a trip to San Diego and Mexico, with stops at Disneyland, Universal Studios, Grand Canyon, Mesa Verde, Santa Fe, Taos, Boulder Colorado and Pierre South Dakota. The boys are wonderful travellers. Having a van helped. Peter’s place was in the back seat. His time was spent Shannara, sleeping, listening to tapes. On occasion, he drove. But the Interstate highways are sometimes crazy and his neck gets sore from the tension. Matthew and Erik had their places in the middle seat. Matthew read Elfstones, listened to Weird Al Yankovic, slept, watched scenery. Erik sang along with Weird Al, slept,  played with a car, making hissing sounds, jiggled his foot on my armrest. No real conflict. Mostly only the anticipation of our next stop, or conversations about what we’d just done.

Peter has grown taller than me. He is lean. His glasses are gone. His hair is a mop. Somedays there are hours of him being neither seen nor heard. He reads on his bed, or plays music on his stereo. Cello is a fixture in his life. He worried early on in the trip about falling behind the others in his quartet: Sara was playing with the National Youth Orchestra, Elation was at a workshop in Montreal. As soon as we were back he resumed practising his studies and quartet pieces. They will play for a wedding in Prince Albert on the 19th and at the National Quartet Competitions in Calgary on the 21st. He’s excited about both. He’ll take the van to PA. The quartet will fly to Calgary. Their tickets are paid for by the National Association. That includes a seat for his cello. They’ll be gone until the following Tuesday. They have rooms at the Olympic Village. He’s pretty self-contained about his thoughts. The more he thinks, the quieter he gets. We have to watch rather than ask if we’re going to learn anything meaningful. He has had a very busy year. The quartet is taking more of his time. It practises frequently now with Mark Rudoff as their coach. He was also in a drama class. They put a performance with Peter as lead. The play is about a boy who trying to write a test, but with all the distractions real and imagined, he gets nothing done. The grandparents were there. We laughed throughout. All his subjects went very well, except for French. He wishes now that he joined the SRC. He also wished he had wrestled. That would have cut into his work. He spent two hours every Wednesday after school at the Y as a guard, and got additional hours guarding or teaching. He drives himself everywhere, easing our schedule. He spends a lot of evenings away with his friends: mostly with the SYO kids, Sara, Elation, Alan, Michael Barth, sometimes Andrew Agrey; sometimes with school friends, Raghu, Stuart, Derek, Joe Woolley. They watch movies, go wall-climbing, play pool, attend string performances at Mayfair United Church or the Centennial Auditorium or Convocation Hall, and bike. Peter has the same sense of humour. He is a great mimic, he has quips, he tells stories about his friends. For example: Just after visiting Cheryl’s grandmother at the Circle Drive Old Folks home, we were departing from the parking lot behind car with a license plate with handicap markings. Thinking aloud of the advantages that designation would bring, I wondered aloud what it would cost. Peter said, ‘An arm and a leg.’ When he’s with us he’s quiet. But he listens carefully to conversations. As soon as our voices are lowered to confidential tones, he positively hones in. His ears have the power of radar dishes. If he’s in another part of the room, we’ll watch his head pivot, his ear train in. About his own affairs, he lets us know only what he wants us to know. If he were to say too much, we’d use it somewhere to embarrass him. He is active in some conversations with his friends or select adults. He says what he has experiences, or he makes jokes. His talking is full of head nodding and smiling. His eight year old swimming students call him Petey. He arrives at class not a moment before it’s supposed to begin in order to escape their clinging on. He told about a 15 year old in his Masters class who has failed twice already, who is very cool while he swims, keeping his head out of the water just so, never straining, well short of a full effort. Peter calls him Chachi after the ‘very cool’ character from Happy Days. Peter’s a recognized asset on Life Guard competition teams, both as a powerful swimmer and quick responses in guarding scenarios. Had he not been away through July, he could have had a full time job with the city.

Matthew has taken on many interests. He still loves both indoor and outdoor soccer. He has settled into the fullback position. He’s fast. He always wants to situate behind the ball, not trusting others to keep it out of the net. His instinct is not to attack upfield. He talks a lot to the other players, helps keep order on the field, is trusted by the others. He is on the Century track club relay team, that includes Joel Dei, Matt Johnson and Garret Separlo. The team is always at or near the best times in the city. Matthew and Garret were offered complementary 1-year memberships. He likes running longer distances too with both the track club and at school: he finished 11th out of all the 13-and-under boys in the city. He attended basketball camp last summer and played both on the school basketball team and the volleyball team. He played hockey through the winter and got a much-prized jersey for Christmas with Norge and the Norwegian flag printed on the front. He also played in the Saskatoon Strings again with Terry Sturge. This year he didn’t miss the city-tour. He played a Popper piece in his very first festival appearance and was highly praised by the adjudicator and Terry and Bernadette. He has developed a comfortable practise regimen. He is therefore very easy to work with. He learns quickly. He now plays a full-size cello. We’re testing a $525 German factory-made bow. Bernadette had kept him at the 3/4 size longer than she kept Peter after learning the technical dangers of playing an instrument that is too large. Matthew on the cello is Matthew with everything he does: deliberate, slow, thoughtful, confident, self-knowing, detailed, purposeful, always enjoying. His work is careworn. He insists on perfection first time, which gives him problems with deadlines or incompleteness. This is often frustrating for us because his work always shows such promise. When Peter expressed interest in participating in the Kinasao Father's Day Bikeathon, Matthew wanted to join. 185 km. Matt Johnson said he’d go too. When Peter discovered he’s booked up that weekend, and Matt Johnson found that his bike is not roadworthy (his money goes into running shoes), Matthew wasn’t deterred. He’d go alone. He got everything together, checked over and over the list of things to bring, hounded us for the things he didn’t have, and raised $400. The day came and off he and the group went. We had planned to drive up the next day. We discovered Matthew just short of PA. He was all alone in a no-man’s-land between the leading cyclists, who were too far ahead to be seen, and the trailing cyclists who we had passed some miles back, riding into a stiff wind, his lips dry, wearing biking gloves and a red bandanna under his helmet. It bothered us that he was unsupervised by any support vehicles on this very busy and at times chaotic highway. We were at Kinasao when he eventually rolled in. We applauded. Erik ran beside. He said that even though he was alone most of the time, he enjoyed the entire ride and wold do it again. The following Sunday at Redeemer Pastor Randy, who had also ridden, asked him to stand and receive recognition for being much younger than anyone else who travelled the entire distance. Matthew’s other big achievement was as Rana the frog in the Lakeridge musical production Rana’s Pond, an environmental story about a frog in a polluted pond. Matthew didn’t consult us about taking the role. He discussed it with his teacher, Pat Jamieson. He tried for the lead and got it. The whole thing was quite amazing to us. He had seen how much singing was involved, and Matthew was always resistant to singing in his natural register because he ‘sounds like a girl’. He growls out the hymns because that’s what boys should sound like. He performed the play twice. He has a lovely soprano voice.
