Christmas holidays. Typically, this is when the holiday relaxation truly begins. By now Cheryl has made supper for three sets of guests, Danny and Joan Isaachsen, her Mom and Dad, and my family. Nothing can be simple.

The boys have been busy all fall. Peter enjoys high school very much, has learned a lot, and works very hard. He has come from having scattered procedures and premature sense of completion to having a solid sense of good work. He has friends now, many who are high achievers, and demonstrate real effort: he is often getting phone-calls at 10 pm from someone still busy working on an assignment. At first he resented the pressure, but one Sunday evening it clicked, his frown was gone, his work was spread out on the dining room table, and his marks have gone up from low 80’s to mid or high 90’s. Cheryl is concerned now that he is now working too hard. She thinks some of his teachers are selfish, self-important boobs with no appreciation for life beyond books in their subject area. I don’t escape the accusations. She has always thought I do too much marking, but has always expressed some sympathy. Now that she is seeing the student side, that my marking represents their labours, she is no longer pretending to take my side. She gives me accusing looks. Fittingly my briefcase is ‘undertaker black.’

Peter has made some good friends at school. Gabe Jutras remains close, though he has gotten ‘girl happy’, in Peter’s words. He has spent some noon hours following Gabe while Gabe follows a girl. In time he laughs Gabe off and finds something else to do. Peter is not girl happy yet. He went to the first school dance, but has not interest in attending more. Girls phone him, but he thinks talking on the phone is a waste of time. Girls from the SYO drop by. Two came with Alan Harrington one evening last week to watch movies. Nothing more was said about it in the days that followed. We tease him, he smiles. He thinks our words are like bait on a hook, but he bumps it aside on his way to something more interesting. More than anything, he is caring about his physical appearance. Cheryl bought two or three fashionable pairs of jeans, t-shirts with Mondetta stitched on the front and button-up long-sleeve shirts with button-down collars, Vans shoes, leather belts, and so on. Adolescent conceits aside, Peter spends most of his social time arranging with Stuart Gander and Max Lingard (two French-immersion students from College Park), Alan Harrington (from SYO), to go tobogganing, paint-balling, playing Sega, watching movies.

Music is a bigger thing to him than ever. Since picking up the bassoon Peter’s beginning in the Grade 9 band has expanded to the Grade 10 and the Senior bands. He is taking lessons from David Steer, a young university student who plays in the Saskatoon Symphony. He gives Peter lots of encouragement. His cello lessons with Terry Sturge are also going well.  Peter has taken these things on as his own. He has built up a solid practise system. Terry held a pre-Christmas recital.  Peter played Allegro Appasionato by Saint-Seans. Pat Deibert accompanied. They practised three times, the first with Terry Sturge for an hour and a half, the second just the two of them, the third at the church. After warming up just before the performance, Peter came to sit with us, shaking his head, thinking it hadn’t gone well. But when the time came to perform, he had lost his nervousness, the timing and dynamics were great, and Peter was totally absorbed. He was thrilled. We were too. That includes his brothers, who were perfectly still during the two hours, and his grandparents.

Matthew is a large boy, broad across the back, rounded and full. He is growing into clothes Peter stopped wearing only two years ago. He’s working hard at school. He is taking accelerated math and enriched work in other areas. He works at everything with care and at his own pace. At the start of the year Matthew had problems concentrating in the classroom. His desk was next to the computer and the teacher's desk. He’s like the guy (Rob Schneider) on Saturday Night Live whose desk is in the copy room, talking up whoever comes in: ‘Ronnieeeee, Ronny to make some copieeeees.’ He has been relocated near a couple of girls who are working on the same programs. He brings home interesting stories. The girls shift their desks close behind his, touch him with their pencils or rulers, pretend they haven’t done anything, say things that are almost inaudible, and ignore him when he turns to see what they are wanting. His work has improved, he has less homework, he is happier.

He has been to the orthodontist several times this fall. He is outfitted with head gear and has to wear it up to ten hours a day. He has tried wearing it at night, but he can’t sleep. The gear is designed like a football helmet face guard. Sleeping on his side pushes it off its required line. He doesn’t sleep well on his back. He puts his hands under his head, or lies on his side facing up. That doesn’t work. Now before we go to bed, we come in and take the gear off, which gives him some hours with it, but allows him to sleep the rest of the night. He spends as much time as possible wearing it in the day, not minding the appearance, or that he talks with a slur.

He is attending a soccer clinic at the Wheatland B building. A new floor has been laid onto the concrete floor, green plastic 6” squares, that provides must better traction. Before, the only way to keep grip on concrete was to apply hairspray to the soles of their shoes. He plays forward with a Division 2 Under-12 Lakeridge team. I am assisting, and learn things about the character of 10 and 11-year old boys as well as myself. The cost of discovery, Fridays at 7:30, is greater than the value.

Matthew is loving cello. He is in Book 4, learning Chanson Triste by Tchaikovsky. Bernadette has planned a recital late in January. He is looking ahead to which instrument he’ll play in the Grade 6 band. He talks most often about the French Horn.

Above all else, he is a dreamy boy. He intensely dislikes tough demanding schedules. He’s out of bed later than everyone. He dislikes fixed sequences, mixing dull routines like dressing with more interesting things, like reading or finding forgotten things under his bed. He spends a lot of time in the bathroom, escaping from something he knows is about to be laid on. I helped him get his room in order. We went through his desk drawers, his chest of drawers. We pulled things out from under his bed and from his closet. We assigned places for things, his art supplies, school books, little artifacts (teeth for instance, and shells and small stones). We assigned drawers for school jeans, school shirts, play clothes, summer clothes, socks and underwear. We put his Lego into containers. Once we set up a pattern, I tried to give him the freedom to do the rest. He is stumped. I put something in his hand, he looks at it, puts it in his other hand, and stalls. I give him a pencil. It should be a half-second decision. When he hesitates, I say, ‘Where should it go?’ He says, ‘I don’t know. Do I put it with my art supplies or with my pens? I use it for school but I draw with it too. Sometimes Erik uses it, and sometimes I use it for cello.’
