We took the boys for check-ups to Dr Lappi’s. Matthew flirted around corners with the receptionist. Both boys provided urine samples in Styrofoam cups that we put into a ‘milk window’. Peter stripped down to his underwear, then hid himself under the paper pulled over the examination table. Matthew pulled off his diaper and remained in that state until the check-up was done. They were calm enough until Lappi stepped into the room. Then they got rambunctious. Peter let himself be examined but said he was worried about a needle. Matthew watched and peed on the floor three times. Each time it was mentioned, Lappi leaned over the table each time to see where it happened, insisting, ‘That’s all right.’ And, ‘Don’t worry about it.’ And, ‘The carpet’s easy to clean.’ His assurances sounded awfully un-assuring.

Matthew proved the rule that bravery is directly related to distance. While he got over his fear of the stethoscope easily enough, the ear probe was a different matter. He fought away from it, tore up the paper on the table, needed to be pinned, screamed, and wouldn’t let Lappi touch his ears. Lappi resorted to distractions. He produced Oscar the Grouch. ‘Matthew, would you like to see inside Oscar’s ears?’ He stopped crying long enough to look into the scope at the spot of light on Oscar’s green fur. Then Lappi said, ‘Fine, now let’s look in your ear.’ Matthew pushed him away and began crying. Lappi reached for him across the table, circled the table several times while reaching, taking stabs at Matthew’s ear but getting his cheek, forehead, nose instead, before giving up. He was no longer calm. He had lost his aplomb. He was panting. It was over and Matthew was suddenly calm. Lappi’s last words about the matter were, ‘If it’s this difficult, there’s not much point in trying. Does he ever seem to not hear you?’ I guess that’s a diagnosis.

We went to Cheryl’s parents for raspberry shortcake. Everyone in the family was there.

Peter learned he needs to tread water for only 30 seconds to earn his red badge. Never has he swum with such intensity. Prior to this, he has had such a fear of deep water he has been unwilling even to let go of the pool edge. Today he swam the entire width on his back, pausing twice.

After supper we drove to the Post Office to mail Cameron Haugrud’s birthday card. We walked in Kiwanis Park, looked at the condominiums by St. Paul’s Cathedral, and passed a group of International Folk dancers. Matthew turned away from us and joined them. After a few minutes we came to the Victoria Avenue bridge. I climbed onto the branch of a tree that reached over the water. Matthew wanted to follow. Peter cried out, ‘Oh Daddy, don’t go any farther. Come back.’ We started across the bridge. Peter made sure someone was holding Matthew’s hand. Lots of boats buzzed around, some pulling skiers. Cheryl refused to look down. ‘I hate this,’ she said. Matthew broke from my grip and Cheryl’s grip and made for the rail. Cheryl groaned each time, stayed near the barrier next to the road, and said, ‘I really can’t stand this.’ We got a little better than half way before turning back. Matthew slid his hand along the railing, scattering dried pigeon droppings. Peter held his hand. Andrew Skarsgard passed us on his bicycle. Once back on the Meewasin trail, Matthew was free. He lagged behind. We continued. He climbed on a park bench and let us go nearly out of sight. Cheryl turned back. He jumped off the bench and swaggered by her side. Somewhere he had found a cigarette butt. He was holding it between two fingers right where they join. Cheryl noticed only when he put it to his mouth for a drag. The boys chased each other on the paths through the lilac bushes. More folk dancers were gathered. A fishing boat passed very slowly, pulling a skier. People just disembarked from the Northcote ferry were scrambling up the steep bank.
