By now we have a routine. I spend part of the morning preparing for the fall. Cheryl takes the boys to the George Ward pool. Peter has his lesson from 9:30 to 10 and Matthew runs on the deck. Peter is learning the t-stroke and swimming with a life-jacket. He is over his timidity. He’s always the first to jump in. Cheryl is sewing a lot and applying countless coats to the picnic table. Matthew gets into whatever he sees. His mood is unvaryingly fine. I swim every day after lunch for 40 – 50 minutes. Then the four of us come and go on chores and errands. After supper we go for a car ride or a bike ride until it’s the boys’ bedtime.

Schedules exist to be upset. From Friday to Tuesday we were up north. Until Monday we worked on the farm building a playhouse, cleaning brush, moving machinery, pruning back trees, hauling brush, building up walls for a gravel pit, and mowing. Matthew has to be watched all the time. A bear is somewhere in the woods. Peter refuses to go to the bathroom without someone near him. He’d rather use an ice-cream pail or pee off the front step. Work starts at 9 and continues until 7, then goes another hour from 8 to 9. Somewhere in there we get some motorcycle riding—Cheryl has learned, but she won’t go out of 1st gear—or bits of reading. Peter wears work gloves and helps where he can, driving the lawn tractor and wagon back and forth between the log pile and chainsaw (which he won’t come near without clamping his hands over his ears). Papa found a swarm of wasps near the house. They have nested behind the asphalt siding. I was stung while in the bush, which was a lesson for Peter. After that he wouldn’t leave the house. Once I came in for water, spent maybe five minutes in the dark, cooling off, before discovering Peter on the sofa with blankets pulled up to his eyes, wearing a crash helmet.

It’s a trick getting Matthew to bed. Friday night I tried sleeping with him. At the first sign of tiredness, which wasn’t until 10:15, we lay him down. He obviously believed we were playing a game. In no time he parted the curtain in the doorway, said ‘Hi,’ and climbed into someone’s lap. This happened a couple of times, until I lay down with him. But I couldn’t keep him down. He stood up, bounced and landed on his bum, and stood again. My efforts to control him weren’t appreciated. He made a sharp complaint and pushed me away. At 11:15 he was still on his feet. I decided I’d let him choose his own time. It wasn’t more than a few minutes after I rolled over before he was sleeping. The next problem was keeping him covered. He must have gotten the impression the bed had been turned 90 degrees. He assumed a position perpendicular to my own. Sometimes his feet were in my face and sometimes his head. I’d wake up with his hair in my nose. Cheryl’s dad spent Saturday and Sunday evening rocking him. Even so he wasn’t sleeping before 11.

We spent a partial day at the Isaachsen farm, along with Betty, Louise and Erling, before heading up to Waskesiu. Inside the Park we were delayed by a buck crossing the highway. Peter had been sleeping, but woke up in a hurry. We went to Bakers’, found a one-room cabin on the lake front (Ireland #35) for $29. We unloaded our things and went for supper to the Park Café. Cheryl and I relaxed while the boys played with the toggles on an idling video game. It was too cool to go to the beach, so we walked around the Townsite. Matthew goes like a car with bad alignment, bad steering, and one flat tire. Whatever our line is, his jerks left. If we followed that line, he’d jerk further left. But by following him we do find things we wouldn’t have noticed, things under rocks, on golf carts, in the back porches of stores, restaurants, on picnic tables (two old women tanning, their poodles leashed, bounding and barking), under the porches of a nightclub just opening its doors. Matthew bellows his greeting at everyone, and waves like he learned on parade day with one or both hands. We headed back to Bakers and walked some more around that area, discovered a playground by the shore. We sat on the teeter-totter (Matthew and I weigh more than Cheryl and Peter), hung from the monkey-bars, climbed a tall slide with a hollow pit instead of sand. We followed the shore. The boys clambered over spruce roots that had been exposed by the waves. While Cheryl and I sat, they spotted each other through the gaps under the roots, kicked sand, threw sticks and stones. Cheryl made a pattern in the sand with a stone that Peter imagined were tracks, and wondered which animal made them. We returned to the car and drove to get Matthew to sleep. That didn’t turn out so well, so we went back to the cabin. While Cheryl rocked him to sleep, Peter and I headed out. Down at the water we found a catamaran pulled onto the shore. In the store Peter insisted I buy myself a book. I know him by now. He is thinking that every dollar I spend on myself has to be matched with a dollar spent on treats for him. As Bernadette Wilson likes to say, ‘Close but no cigar.’ We put Peter to bed on a pull-out couch. Matthew was put on blankets and fenced in with suitcases and bags and cushions. Our bed was a single. We read awhile. Once the lights were out, it didn’t take us long to learn that the only way for one to turn was for both to turn together. That didn’t work, and we didn’t get any sleep. Some time before morning Peter fell on the floor and tipped a suitcase onto Matthew. Which didn’t disturb Matthew’s sleep. Peter and I snuck out, showered, and found large rodents living under the cabin. Peter squatted and offered pieces of bread. A fellow saw what he was doing and brought him a handful of peanuts. Between these and the bread, one came near enough almost to touch. Just then our cabin door banged open and Matthew appeared.

‘Hi Eeda,’ he yelled.

The animal vanished. The guy who provided Peter with peanuts got up from his squatting position and walked away.

Matthew waved. ‘Bye,’ he yelled. ‘Bye-bye.’

We paid our $29 at the office, drove to the Park Café for coffee, and were set up on the beach by 10:30. Cheryl and I took turns watching the boys. I walked Matthew to the breakwater. He slipped twice in slimy water. Which surprised him. ‘Ouss, ouss,’ he said, again and again for the rest of the walk. We came upon a perch suspended a couple of feet from the surface.

Pointing, Matthew said, ‘Whass-a?’

‘A perch, Matthew,’ I said.

‘Whass-a?’

‘A perch.’

‘Whass-a?’

We remained there until 3:30. Peter had spent until then in the water, going around with small bands of kids, herding the minnows, trying to catch some in their pails, and digging the wet sand. Cheryl sunburned her back. We drove to PA, stopped at Venice’s for pizza and spaghetti. We were hungry, but would have settled for the iced water. On the drive home the sun continued to burn. One’s skin feels clad in long underwear from ankles to wrists. The boys fussed.
